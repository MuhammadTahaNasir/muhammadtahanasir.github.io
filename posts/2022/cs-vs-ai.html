Why I Chose Computer Science Over a Specialized AI Degree

March 15, 2022 • 5 min • Muhammad Taha Nasir

Artificial Intelligence is everywhere.

Open YouTube, and you'll find another video predicting that AI will replace programmers. Browse Twitter, and someone is showing a neural network generating artwork that looks almost human. University brochures suddenly have "Artificial Intelligence" printed across their front pages, promising students they're stepping into the future.

It feels like the entire technology industry has collectively agreed on one thing: AI is the future.

Which naturally leads to another question: If Artificial Intelligence is the future, why would anyone choose Computer Science instead?

For the past few weeks, I've been asking myself exactly that.

Every career discussion eventually reaches the same conclusion. AI is growing. Companies are investing billions into research. Universities are launching specialized degrees. Everyone seems convinced that machine learning engineers will become one of the most valuable professionals of the next decade.

So shouldn't I just study AI?

It sounds logical. It sounds efficient. And honestly, it sounds exciting.

But the more I researched universities, degree plans, and the engineers building the software I admired, the more one thing kept bothering me.

Almost none of them started with AI. They started with Computer Science.

That felt... strange.

Why would people building tomorrow's technology spend years learning things like linked lists, memory allocation, operating systems, and computer architecture instead of jumping directly into neural networks?

There had to be a reason. So I started digging.

The Hype Is Real

Artificial Intelligence isn't just another trend. For the first time in years, it genuinely feels like software is entering a completely new era.

Recommendation systems are getting smarter. Voice assistants are improving. Computer vision is becoming surprisingly accurate. Self-driving cars are no longer science fiction.

Every week seems to introduce another breakthrough. It's hard not to be fascinated.

And maybe that's exactly why so many students (including me) are tempted to skip straight to AI.

Why spend months learning pointers in C++ when you could be training neural networks? Why study operating systems when you could build intelligent applications?

At first glance, the answer seems obvious. Skip the boring part. Start with the exciting part.

But engineering rarely rewards shortcuts.

Looking Beyond the Buzzwords

The more I explored AI, the more I noticed something interesting. Every explanation eventually relied on concepts I didn't fully understand yet.

People talked about optimization. Algorithms. Memory efficiency. Parallel computing. Data structures. Complexity analysis. Distributed systems.

These weren't AI concepts. These were Computer Science concepts.

It felt like trying to understand modern architecture without first understanding how foundations support a building. The skyscraper is impressive. But nobody talks about the concrete underneath.

The higher I looked, the more I realized everything eventually pointed downward. Toward the fundamentals.

Computer Science Isn't About Programming

This was probably my biggest misconception. I used to think Computer Science meant learning programming languages. Learn C++. Learn Java. Build software. Graduate. Simple.

But that's only the surface. Programming is just the tool.

Computer Science is really the science behind why software works in the first place:

  • Why one algorithm finishes in milliseconds while another takes minutes.
  • Why memory behaves differently on the stack and the heap.
  • Why operating systems schedule processes the way they do.
  • Why databases can retrieve millions of records almost instantly.
  • Why distributed systems remain reliable even when machines fail.

Those questions fascinated me far more than I expected. Because once you understand them, every technology built on top suddenly starts making a lot more sense. Including AI.

A Thought Experiment

Imagine two students graduating five years from now:

  • Student A: Spent university mastering the latest AI frameworks.
  • Student B: Spent the same time building a deep understanding of algorithms, systems, networking, databases, and software engineering while learning AI alongside those fundamentals.

Now imagine the industry changes. New frameworks replace old ones. Different models dominate the market. Entire workflows evolve.

Which engineer adapts faster?

I don't know the answer with certainty. But I have a feeling fundamentals survive longer than tools. History certainly seems to agree.

Technology Never Stops Moving

Ten years ago, mobile development dominated conversations. Then cloud computing. Then DevOps. Now AI.

Technology doesn't stand still. Every generation believes it has found the final answer. Then something new appears.

That's why I'm becoming increasingly convinced that chasing technologies isn't nearly as valuable as understanding the principles underneath them. Languages evolve. Libraries disappear. Frameworks get rewritten. Good engineering doesn't.

So... Why Computer Science?

Because I don't just want to know how to build software. I want to understand why it works.

I want to understand why algorithms scale. Why systems fail. Why memory matters. Why operating systems behave the way they do. Why networks introduce latency. Why software architecture determines whether an application survives growth or collapses under it.

Those ideas don't become obsolete every few years. They're timeless. And maybe that's exactly what makes Computer Science such a powerful foundation.

Not because it's trendy. But because it isn't.

Reality Check

Will studying Computer Science automatically make someone a better AI engineer? Of course not.

It still takes curiosity. Practice. Projects. Failures. Thousands of lines of code. Late nights debugging problems that probably shouldn't exist.

But if AI really becomes the future everyone predicts, I want to enter that future standing on a solid foundation instead of chasing every new headline.

Final Thoughts

Maybe I'll read this a few years from now and completely disagree with myself. Maybe AI programs will become the standard. Maybe universities will redesign how engineering is taught.

Or maybe I'll discover that this decision quietly shaped everything that came afterward.

Right now, I don't know.

What I do know is this:

Artificial Intelligence may be the destination. But Computer Science feels like the best place to begin the journey.
