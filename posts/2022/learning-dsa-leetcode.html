Breaking into DSA with LeetCode: The Real Struggle Behind the Grind

September 20, 2022 • 3 min • Muhammad Taha Nasir

Real growth isn't loud. Sometimes, it looks like one failed submission after another, and still choosing to try again the next day.

So, I'm back after a few months and starting my Data Structures and Algorithms (DSA) journey on LeetCode. After choosing CS over a specialized degree and getting comfortable with Python thanks to Zia Sir, I thought LeetCode would be the natural next step. Real problems, real logic, real improvement. But man, this rabbit hole goes deep.

The Starting Point

When you first open LeetCode, it looks deceptively straightforward. You click on "Two Sum", read the prompt, and think, "How hard can this really be?"

Then you hit "Submit" and get hit with a Time Limit Exceeded (TLE) error. That is when the reality of algorithmic time complexity hits you for the first time.

Writing code that passes basic test cases is easy. Writing code that scales across 100,000 items under strict time and memory constraints requires a fundamental mindset shift.

The Reality of Struggling

You don't just learn DSA; you fight with it. Some days, I spend hours on a single problem, only to realize I misunderstood the boundary conditions entirely.

Array indices throw me off, keeping track of zero-indexed offsets and off-by-one errors is a constant headache. Nested loops turn into mental mazes. Debugging has become my daily routine. But every "Wrong Answer" screen teaches me something vital. Struggling isn't weakness; it is proof of real learning.

Two-Sum Evolution (From O(N²) to O(N)):

# Brute Force O(N^2) -> TLE on Large Inputs
def twoSum_slow(nums, target):
    for i in range(len(nums)):
        for j in range(i + 1, len(nums)):
            if nums[i] + nums[j] == target:
                return [i, j]

# Optimized O(N) Hash Map -> Accepted 
def twoSum_fast(nums, target):
    seen = {}
    for i, num in enumerate(nums):
        diff = target - num
        if diff in seen:
            return [seen[diff], i]
        seen[num] = i

Python as a Weapon and a Constraint

Python is my default language from institute classes with Zia Sir. It's expressive, quick to write, and lets me prototype ideas rapidly. But LeetCode doesn't care about pretty syntax. It cares about logic, time complexity, and memory overhead.

Even simple tasks like tracking frequency maps or sliding window ranges require clear algorithmic thinking. Zia Sir showed me Python's elegance, but now I see it's about building a sharper problem-solving mind.

Algorithmic Complexity & Scale

Understanding Big O notation isn't just academic theory; it is the difference between a submission that passes in 30ms and one that times out after 3.0s.

Moving your mindset from "How do I solve this?" to "How do I solve this in optimal time and memory?" is the core transformation every developer undergoes.

What Makes This So Hard

Let's be real: college mock exams and coursework deadlines kill focus. As an engineering student, balancing midterm papers, lab assignments, and daily LeetCode practice is tough.

Being inconsistent is hard to admit, but pulling late nights between exam prep and algorithmic problems tests your endurance. Either way, staying disciplined through academic pressure is part of the growth process.

The Road Ahead

I'm not at a "100-Day LeetCode Streak" yet, and that is okay. I have started, and I am not quitting. After midterms, my plan is simple: solve one problem a day, break complex DSA topics into manageable bits, and keep building consistency.

The real win isn't solving problems instantly; it's refusing to give up when a problem feels impossible.

Real growth isn't loud. Sometimes, it looks like one failed submission after another, and still choosing to try again the next day.
